Thunderhawk

Dorney Park · Allentown, Pennsylvania

By the Coaster Map editorial team · Last verified 10 Nov 2026 · How we verify

Height requirement: 48 in (122 cm); top speed 45 mph; 80 ft tall; 0 inversions; opened 1924.

Thunderhawk is a wood sit-down roller coaster at Dorney Park in Allentown, Pennsylvania, built by Philadelphia Toboggan Coasters, Inc. It first opened on 30 Mar 1924 and was previously known as Coaster. The ride stands 80 ft tall, drops 65 ft, reaches 45 mph and runs 2,767 ft of track. It is a wooden coaster, with a wooden track structure. It does not turn riders upside down.

What riding Thunderhawk is like

With 2,767 ft of track for a 80 ft structure, Thunderhawk is a long, low-slung ride: most of the time is spent carving through turns and small hills rather than on one big plunge.

Opened in 1924, it is the oldest of the 8 coasters we list at Dorney Park. It has run under other names (Coaster), so older guides and photos may use those.

Its 48 in minimum is the lowest of any coaster at Dorney Park (tied with 3 others), which makes it a natural first coaster there. Among thrill-class coasters on Coaster Map the median ride-alone minimum is about 48 in (122 cm), so this rule is about typical for its class.

Going by RCDB's map positions, Thunderhawk sits on the west side of Dorney Park's coaster line-up. The nearest other coaster is Possessed, roughly 120 m away (52 in minimum), so the two are easy to ride back to back. Next closest is Steel Force at about 130 m. How intense is it?

Moderate (thrill)

RCDB classes it as a thrill coaster; Dorney Park rates it 4 out of 5 on its own thrill scale.

Who is it for?

It suits riders who enjoy speed and turns but may not want the biggest drops in the park and riders who prefer not to go upside down. Riders must be at least 48 in (122 cm) tall.

Best seat to ride

Pick the front row for the view and the back rows for airtime: the train drags the rear cars over every crest faster than the front.

How Thunderhawk compares

On our intensity ranking Thunderhawk is number 6 of 8 at Dorney Park, where 1 is the most intense. It is the 6th tallest of the park's 8 coasters with a listed height.

Frequently asked questions

What is the height requirement for Thunderhawk?

Riders must be at least 48 in (122 cm) tall to ride Thunderhawk, according to Dorney Park, official ride page (checked 10 Nov 2026). Requirements can change, so confirm at the ride entrance.

How tall is Thunderhawk?

Thunderhawk is 80 ft tall, with a drop of 65 ft, according to RCDB.

How fast does Thunderhawk go?

Its top speed is 45 mph (72 km/h), according to RCDB.

Does Thunderhawk go upside down?

No. It has no inversions.

Is Thunderhawk scary?

It depends on the rider, but on intensity: RCDB classes it as a thrill coaster; Dorney Park rates it 4 out of 5 on its own thrill scale. Expect a lively ride that stops short of the most extreme coasters.

When did Thunderhawk open?

Thunderhawk opened on 30 Mar 1924 (previously known as Coaster).

Who built Thunderhawk?

Thunderhawk was manufactured by Philadelphia Toboggan Coasters, Inc.

How long is the ride on Thunderhawk?

RCDB lists a ride time of 1 minute 18 seconds over 2,767 ft of track.

How does Thunderhawk get up its first hill?

RCDB lists a chain lift hill; it has no launch.

How many people ride Thunderhawk at once?

Each train carries 24 riders, according to RCDB.

What restraints does Thunderhawk use?

RCDB lists: single position lap bar, headrest, seatbelt.

Is Thunderhawk good for first-time riders?

On the numbers: RCDB classes it as thrill; it has no inversions; it tops out at 45 mph; the minimum height is 48 in. Most first-timers start with a family-class ride with no inversions, so this is better as a step up than a first coaster.

Is Thunderhawk at Dorney Park the same as Thunderhawk at Michigan's Adventure?

They share a name but are separate rides; the Dorney Park ride opened in 1924 and the Michigan's Adventure ride in 2008; Dorney Park sets 48 in and Michigan's Adventure sets 52 in; RCDB lists heights of 80 ft and 109.3 ft.
